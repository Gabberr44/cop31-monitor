# Launch of the Global Carbon Budget 2026
**When:** 10/11, time n/a (event in the UNFCCC programme [2]; press release embargoed to 10 Nov) · **Where:** Antalya EXPO Center · **Organiser:** University of Exeter, with the University of East Anglia (UEA), CICERO (climate research centre), LMU Munich and the Global Carbon Project: over 140 scientists from more than 90 institutions [1][2]<br>

## In brief
**The Global Carbon Project** presented its annual scientific estimate of global CO₂ emissions, a preprint in *Earth System Science Data* with a peer-reviewed paper to follow [1]. It matters because it shows a first fall in fossil emissions, but also a 1.5 °C budget that is almost used up.

## Key points
- **The Global Carbon Project** puts 2026 fossil CO₂ emissions at 38.2 bn t, down 0.4% ✅ [1]. This would be the first fall since 2020 ✅ [1].
- **The authors** link the fall partly to the oil price shock from the Iran conflict ✅ [1]. The other part comes from the growth of solar, wind and electric vehicles ✅ [1]. By fuel: oil −1.9%, gas −0.3%, coal +0.6% ✅ [1].
- **The report** shows total emissions rising to 43.3 bn t, from 42.8 in 2025, including land use and deforestation ✅ [1].
- **The main emitters** move as follows: China −0.9%, United States −1.3%, European Union −1.5%, Japan −1%, India +4.4% ✅ [1].
- **The Global Carbon Project** puts the remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C at 150 bn t, under four years at current emissions ✅ [1].
- **The report** estimates that novel (technological) CO₂ removal offsets less than 30 minutes of annual fossil emissions ✅ [1].

## Announcements and numbers
- **Solar and wind** avoided 4.1 bn t of emissions in 2025 and a projected 4.6 bn t in 2026 ✅ [1].
- **59 countries** have emissions below 2019 while their economies grew ✅ [1].

## Quotes
- "A drop in emissions in one year is not a peak unless it is driven by structural change." — **Glen Peters, CICERO** [1]

## Relevance for the VSME
**Low** — a macro figure, not a rule, but a reminder that the 1.5 °C budget is running out and that pressure from customers and banks on targets and reductions is increasing. The CO₂-removal figure is a reminder that direct reductions matter more than credits.
